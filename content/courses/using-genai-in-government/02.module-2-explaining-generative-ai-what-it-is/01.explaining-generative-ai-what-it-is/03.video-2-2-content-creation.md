# Video 2.2 | Content Creation

This segment showcases GenAI's content creation capabilities with examples such as drafting letters, generating interview questions, and creating images. The video emphasizes GenAI's dramatic leap forward compared to traditional tools and its potential to save time and improve outcomes in government work.

### Audio description transcript - Content Creation

*\[Visual description]:* Text appears: The InnovateUS Logo.

*\[Visual description]:* Title card: Responsible AI for Public Professionals: Using GenAI in Government.

*\[Visual description]:* Title card: Content Creation.

*\[Visual description]:* Text appears: Ariana Escanlante, presenter, InnovateUS.

It’s helpful to think about Generative AI’s capabilities in two broad categories: content creation and content analysis. We’ll start with content creation.

*\[Visual description]:* Content Creation is shown as a woman sitting behind a computer streaming out a torrent of music notes, scripts, and videos. Content Analysis is shown as a torrent of creative information being funneled into a lightbulb held by a woman.

A common example: government agencies regularly send letters and emails to residents about their rights and benefits.

*\[Visual description]:* A mailbox containing letters reading “Tax Notice” and “Planning Permission Approval” is shown.

Getting those communications right matters enormously—when we don’t, people can lose access to critical services.

Yet translating complex policies into clear, actionable language is difficult.

In New Jersey, the Department of Labor used a Generative AI tool to help rewrite dozens of communications templates for benefit recipients.

*\[Visual description]:* The Department of Labor office is shown.

Staff carefully reviewed every version to ensure accuracy. The result: not only did this save time and reduce costs, it led to a 75% decrease in the time it took residents to read and respond.

*\[Visual description]:* A Project pipeline is shown with the following title: How New Jersey is using GenAI. The pipeline has the following steps: Existing letters, Gen AI, Rewritten letters, Review and revision, and Final letter.

In practice, one of the most common uses of Generative AI is co-creating first drafts of memos, testimony, speeches, and other documents.

*\[Visual description]:* a stylised image of AI with links to Memos, Testimony, and Speeches.

The goal isn’t to replace human judgment, but to accelerate the process and improve quality.

Imagine you need to interview a job candidate—you could generate a starter set of interview questions to then revise.

*\[Visual description]:* A user prompts the following question to the AI tool Claude: Give me a set of questions to ask a candidate in a job interview.

*\[Visual description]:* Text appears: Disclaimer: The commercial tools mentioned in this course are for illustrative purposes and do not imply endorsement by InnovateUS.

Generative AI can also create images. For example, you might describe an image for a presentation on wildfire management strategies, and the tool will generate one. If it’s not quite right, you can refine it iteratively: “Make this simpler,” or “Use a more illustrative style.”

*\[Visual description]:* A GenAI tool is asked to create the spoken prompt, it creates images showing wildfires and changes these as the user dictates.

Some tools can also generate video. Platforms like Canva can turn text into short videos.

*\[Visual description]:* Canva’s website and a Synthesia-generated video are shown.

Tools like Synthesia can generate videos like the trailer we made for this course.

Now it’s your turn to try.

Go to a Generative AI tool approved for use in your organization. Choose a topic you know well so you can evaluate the results. Do not include any sensitive or personally identifiable information.

*\[Visual description]:* The AI tool Claude is shown being used as an example for this try it yourself exercise.

Ask it to draft a professional memo on your topic. Before you begin, think about the key points you want included.

Once you have a draft, review it critically:

Does anything look incorrect or out of place?

Is this how you would have written it?

How does it compare to drafting from scratch?

Next, ask the tool to rewrite the memo at a ninth-grade reading level.

Finally, ask how the memo could be improved.

Next, we’ll move from content creation to content analysis.

*\[Visual description]:* The two images from the beginning of the video, representing Content Creation and Content Analysis, are shown with an arrow pointing from Content Creation to Content Analysis.
