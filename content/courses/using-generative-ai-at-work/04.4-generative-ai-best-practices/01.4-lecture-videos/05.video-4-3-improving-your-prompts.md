*\[Visual description]:* Text appears: Ariana Escanlante, presenter, InnovateUS.

Welcome back. In the last video, we covered the core components of effective prompting. Now let’s look at five techniques to improve your results.

Remember: The more clearly you guide  Generative AI, the better the outcome.

First, instead of asking for a complete report, outline the steps you want the model to follow. Structured prompts lead to clearer, more logical outputs. If there are rules the GenAI shouldn't break, or should always do, those can be specified.

Second, if you’re unsure how to frame a request, ask the tool what additional information it needs. This can help clarify the task and improve the result.

Third, work iteratively. If the first response isn’t right, ask for changes—more concise, more formal, more detailed. You don’t need to start over.

Fourth, for more involved tasks, break the process into stages—brainstorm, outline, draft, revise. Use each output as input for the next step.

Finally, Generative AI tools can only process a limited amount of text at once, known as the context window. If your input is too long, earlier content may be truncated, and even within the limit, models can struggle to use all information effectively. They tend to perform best on material at the beginning and end of a prompt, and may underuse information in the middle. When in doubt, simplify your input or start a new conversation.

Now try it yourself. Choose a work-related task on an approved platform and apply each of these techniques. Notice how the output improves as you refine your approach. Use only public or non-sensitive information.

Be direct. You can say, “That’s not quite right—here’s what I need.” The tool won’t take offense—it will adjust.

At the same time, remember that these tools are designed to be people-pleasing and may reinforce your assumptions.

*\[Visual description]:* Image appears: a screenshot of the Science article titled: Sycophantic AI decreases prosocial intentions and promotes dependence.

One research study tested leading models and found every one to be agreeable to a fault.

So don’t take the first answer at face value. Ask follow-ups, challenge the response, and request counterarguments. Treat the interaction as a dialogue, not a confirmation.

Analytical tasks—like summarizing or comparing documents—require clear instructions and sufficient context. Creative tasks—like drafting speeches—benefit from specifying tone and role. Remember to treat outputs as a starting point that requires careful review, not a final product.

Prompting is a skill that improves with practice. Keep a personal library of prompts that work well. Many teams build shared prompt libraries and InnovateUS offers a weekly prompting lab so you can learn from others

At its core, effective prompting is about forming a productive dialogue with the tool—guiding it toward results that meet your needs.
