# Video 5: Identifying Participants

This video explores the tradeoffs between self-selection and targeted selection, and between representative and non-representative approaches. It explains how different goals call for different participants and shows how you can use AI to help identify gaps in representation.

\[Visual description]: Title appears: Matching Participants to Purpose.

Identifying participants is about recognizing those with important lived experience, knowledge, or skills. Different goals call for different participants. One key choice is between self-selection and targeted selection. Self-selection means opening participation to anyone who wants to take part.

\[Visual description]: The Finland Citizens' Initiative website appears alongside the GovLab project page for it.

Finland's Citizens' Initiative is a strong example: any adult citizen can propose legislation, and Parliament must consider proposals that reach 50,000 signatures. This approach channels public energy into real policy change. Targeted selection involves deliberately recruiting participants. It may call for lived experience, specific interests, or a representative sample.

In Central Oregon, a civic assembly on youth homelessness selected participants to mirror the county's demographics, an approach known as sortition, helping ensure its recommendations reflected the full community rather than just those who would have volunteered.

As we saw in Germany, the most effective engagement processes often combine methods to capture the benefits of each.

Finally, consider diversity of viewpoints, not just demographics. Bringing together people who think differently and prioritize different values leads to stronger deliberation, more collective intelligence, and better outcomes. Be intentional about your choices and transparent about how and why participants were selected.

\[Visual description]: Title appears: Using AI to Improve Participant Identification.

You can use AI as a brainstorming partner to help identify which communities and perspectives should be represented, and where you may have gaps. Or use it to review participant lists or survey responses from past engagements to spot people or viewpoints that have been included or left out in the past.

\[Visual description]: Title appears: Risks in Participant Identification.

Several risks deserve particular attention. Self-selection bias. When participation is open to anyone, the people who show up tend to be those who are already engaged, more educated, politically active, and connected. Without deliberate efforts to broaden who participates, self-selected engagement can reinforce existing inequalities. Representative selection may exclude relevant expertise. Representative selection is often resource-intensive and logistically complex, which can put it out of reach for institutions with limited budgets or capacity. Costs may limit the scale and scope of who participates, missing out on the opportunity to be more inclusive. Sortition may also include those with limited knowledge of or interest in the topic.

Some are experimenting with AI to simulate public opinion by predicting how different groups might respond. While you can test your messaging with an AI tool, these "silicon samples" are not a substitute for real participation.

\[Visual description]: Image appears: Screenshot of an arXiv paper, Title: Large Language Models Do Not Simulate Human Psychology.

Also, be careful not to "stack the room" only with those who agree with you, lest your engagement lose credibility and value.

We are co-designing an AI tool to help families in California understand and engage with their children's individualized education programs, or IEPs, which are often long, technical, and difficult to access, especially for non-English-speaking parents and families.
