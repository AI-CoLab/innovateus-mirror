# Video 10: Using What the Group Creates

This video addresses the most consequential step in engagement: turning input into action and communicating the results. It covers how to assess what you can realistically act on, how to build transparency into decisionmaking, and how you can use AI to support real-time feedback, contribution tracing, and ongoing reporting.

Informally, it had long been called the Hudson River Bridge, a name widely liked.

In response, the agency held a public vote. Tens of thousands of people participated and overwhelmingly voted for the Hudson River Bridge.

But the outcome was ignored. The Port Authority proceeded with the name George Washington Bridge and declined to explain its decision.

\[Visual description]: A black and white image of three suited men smiling while reaching out their arms is shown.

The vote had not been a genuine invitation to participate. It was an unsuccessful attempt to manage public criticism.

Nearly a century later, this story still resonates because it captures a central failure of public engagement: asking for input and then disregarding it. When institutions collect contributions they cannot or will not act on, they do not just waste people's time, they damage trust.

\[Visual description]: Title appears: What You’ll Learn.

In this module, you'll learn how to close the loop: how to turn input into action, and how to show people that their participation mattered. The value of public engagement lies not in collecting input, but in using it. The steps we've discussed throughout this course are designed to lead to this moment. Clear ownership, well-defined tasks, and a strong review plan all increase the likelihood that the input you collect can inform real decisions.

Before you begin, it can help to secure a clear commitment from leadership about how results will be used. This does not mean adopting every suggestion, but it does mean defining upfront what will trigger a response. After the engagement, start by assessing what you can realistically act on.

\[Visual description]: Two arrows appear. Arrow one is labeled: Secure a clear commitment from leadership about how results will be used. Arrow two is labeled: Assess what you can realistically act on.

Consider your budget, legal authority, institutional priorities, and timeline. Some ideas may need to be refined, translated into policy language, scoped to fit available resources, or adapted to meet legal requirements. Others may fall outside your capacity and require partnerships to implement. What matters is making deliberate and transparent decisions about what to act on, what falls outside your scope, and setting expectations upfront.

Just as important is communicating the results.

\[Visual description]: A third arrow appears, labeled: Communicate the results.

Show participants how their input influenced the outcome. A simple framework like "We asked, You said, We did" can make this clear.

\[Visual description]: The Consult.gov website appears, containing the exact mentioned quote.

Be equally transparent about what was not done and why. You can also make results visible over time. Public dashboards, progress updates, or follow-up convenings help participants see how their contributions are translated into action.

\[Visual description]: An example report table is shown indicating lowering emissions between 2019 and 2023.

Closing the loop is not a one-time step. It requires ongoing effort to track what changed, assess whether the engagement led to meaningful outcomes, and understand how participants experienced the process. Trust grows when people see that their input made a difference.

\[Visual description]: Title appears: Using AI to Help Close the Loop.

You can use AI to help close the loop by communicating results back to participants faster, more clearly, and at greater scale. You can use AI to provide real-time feedback by surfacing key themes during or immediately after an engagement. Participants can see how their input is being understood while the process is still underway. You can use AI to produce clearer and more engaging reporting. Use it to help you draft clear and simple summaries and updates that explain what was heard and how it informed decisions. Rather than static reports, you can present findings through interactive summaries, visualizations, or question-and-answer interfaces that allow people to explore what the group contributed and what resulted.

\[Visual description]: Title appears: Using AI to Help Close the Loop.

You can also use AI to trace contributions. Emerging approaches allow institutions to show how specific inputs were reflected in the final outcome, giving participants a clearer sense of how their voice contributed to the final result. Finally, you can use AI to support ongoing feedback. By analyzing input over time, you can track how priorities shift, monitor satisfaction, and connect engagement to measurable changes in services or policy. Used well, these approaches make participation visible. They help people see not just that input was collected, but how it was used, reinforcing trust and making engagement more meaningful over time.

\[Visual description]: Title appears: Risks to Watch For.

A few risks deserve attention. First, collecting input without the will to act on it. As the George Washington Bridge story shows, asking for input after decisions have already been made undermines trust more than not asking at all. Second, failing to account for political and institutional constraints.

\[Visual description]: A DW article is shown, titled: France’s citizen climate assembly: A failed experiment?

Promises made during engagement may not survive competing priorities or external pressures, leading to outcomes that fall short of expectations and damage credibility. Third, collecting input without a plan to use it. Without a process for reviewing, evaluating, and responding to contributions, even strong participation will not lead to impact. Fourth, designing a process that produces unusable input.

\[Visual description]: A list of 5 points that currently limit engagement in Madrid: 1: Lack of youth engagement. 2: Lack of engagement among older people. 3: Lack of engagement among organizations. 4: Underrepresentation of women and girls. 5: Declining proposals, increasing processes.

Institutions may be unable to act on vague or off-topic submissions, even when there's a genuine will to do so. Finally, failing to communicate results. Even when input does influence decisions, participants may never know it. Without a visible connection between what people contributed and what happened as a result, participation can feel meaningless. In every case, the result is the same: when contributions do not lead to visible action, trust declines, and institutions lose access to the insight they set out to gather.
