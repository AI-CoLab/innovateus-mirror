# Video 1: Why Engagement Matters Today

This video explains how well-designed public engagement improves the legitimacy and effectiveness of decision-making for institutions, leaders, and the public. It illustrates how practitioners around the world are already using AI to analyze input, model policy options, and make participation more accessible — and sets the stage for the practical framework that follows.

\[Visual description]: Various videos of pets, people voting, and politicians are shown.

This is what democratic participation can look like.

But stories of citizens shaping how institutions decide are vanishingly rare, even in most democracies. Across 24 nations, three out of four residents say elected officials don't care what they think. Done well, engagement improves both the legitimacy and effectiveness of decision-making. For institutions, engagement surfaces expertise, experience and opinions to improve the quality of decision-making. For leaders, public input enhances the legitimacy and effectiveness of their actions and strengthens the sense that leaders are listening and responsive. And for the public, it creates a genuine opportunity to shape the decisions that affect their lives.

\[Visual description]: This section is presented by Beth.

Public engagement is hard. When everyone can speak, institutions struggle to listen. I learned this lesson during the 2008 presidential transition.

\[Visual description]: The Citizen’s Briefing Book from May 2009 is shown.

The incoming administration invited Americans to submit ideas for the first 100 days. More than 125,000 people responded with over 44,000 ideas. Making sense of it all was impossible.

\[Visual description]: Image appears: A screenshot of a GOV.UK article, Title: Government-built "Humphrey" AI tool reviews responses to consultation for first time, in bid to save millions.

It's no wonder the U.K. Cabinet Office estimates that a consultation attracting 30,000 responses requires a team of around 25 analysts for three months to analyze the data and write the report. While many institutions want to engage, the challenge is how to do it well with limited time, budgets and growing demands.

You can use AI to make engagement easier, faster and more accessible. Around the world, practitioners are already using these tools in different ways.

A few cautions before we go further. First, before using AI tools at work, check with your agency AI or IT lead for the most current information about your organization's policies. Second, AI cannot substitute for human engagement. Conversation, trust and judgment are not inefficiencies. Used well, AI frees up time to do more of that work. Used poorly, it undermines it. Third, we don't recommend a single engagement platform or AI tool. We will show you examples with a variety of common tools. Finally, AI has limitations. Its outputs depend on how it is used. We'll address these risks throughout the course. If you're new to these tools, we recommend starting with the AI basics course linked in the materials.
