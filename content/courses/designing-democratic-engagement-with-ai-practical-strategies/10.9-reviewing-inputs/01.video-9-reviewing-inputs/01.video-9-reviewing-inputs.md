# Video 9: Reviewing Inputs

This video walks through how to design a review process — from setting clear evaluation criteria to deciding who reviews contributions. It explains how you can use AI to support analysis through deduplication, summarization, topic modeling, sentiment analysis, and criteria-based classification, while emphasizing that human judgment must guide final decisions.

\[Visual description]: Title appears: Designing Your Review Process.

Start with your values and goals. What are you trying to achieve? The answer should shape how you evaluate input. If your goal is to generate innovative ideas, you may prioritize originality and potential impact. If you're shaping policy, feasibility and alignment with institutional authority may matter more. If you're trying to understand community needs, representativeness and breadth may be key. From there, establish a small set of clear evaluation criteria that define what good looks like.

Common examples include: Feasibility. Can this idea be implemented with available resources? Impact. Does it address a significant need or affect many people? Sustainability. Can it be maintained over time? Legality and authority. Is it within your organization's power to act?

You can also support participants in meeting your criteria. Workshops, guidance, or feedback can help people strengthen their contributions before evaluation, ensuring that good ideas are not overlooked simply because they are poorly presented. Finally, be transparent. Explain how input will be evaluated and who will be involved. When participants understand how their contributions will be evaluated, they can tailor their input accordingly, and they are more likely to trust the process.

\[Visual description]: Title appears: Organizing Input For Review.

Before contributions can be analyzed, they need to be organized: grouped, summarized, or structured so they can be reviewed effectively.

\[Visual description]: Title appears: Goal is to create a clear picture of what people are saying.

The goal at this stage is not to decide which ideas are best, but to create a clear picture of what people are saying. This may involve identifying common themes, grouping similar responses, or distinguishing among different types of contributions.

\[Visual description]: Title appears: How You Can Use AI to Support Analysis.

AI can support both organizing and analyzing input, helping your team move from large volumes of submissions to clear, actionable decisions. A useful starting point is duplicate and template-based submissions. You can use AI to identify duplicate or near-identical submissions. Coordinated submissions are common in large engagements. This allows you to reduce repetition while still tracking the level of support behind an idea. From there, you can use AI to generate summaries of submissions, giving your team a readable overview without needing to review every response individually. The most useful summaries go beyond surface-level themes to capture the underlying arguments, what people are proposing, why, and with what reasoning.

You can use topic modeling to analyze patterns in how words appear together across submissions to identify clusters of related ideas. AI can allow you to group similar submissions based on meaning rather than keywords, allowing you to map patterns in the conversation, even when participants use different language to describe the same issue. You can then refine those themes through human review. Through sentiment analysis, AI can understand whether responses are broadly positive, negative, or mixed, helping you understand how people feel about an issue.

You can use AI to apply your evaluation criteria more consistently. It can classify submissions, for example, identifying which proposals are feasible, within scope, or aligned with policy priorities, and generate rankings or scores as a starting point for review. You can also use AI to compare responses, highlighting differences in views across groups. In practice, the most effective approach combines methods. You might review a sample of submissions manually to define categories and criteria, use AI to organize and classify the full data set, and then apply human judgment to interpret and act on the results.

At every stage, remember that organizing and evaluating input is not neutral. The way submissions are grouped, filtered, and prioritized shapes what decision-makers see. AI can help teams navigate large data sets, but its outputs should always be treated as a starting point for human analysis, not final decisions.

\[Visual description]: Title appears: Risks in Review.

First, not all input should be treated equally. A large number of identical comments may signal strong public feeling, but a smaller number of well-informed contributions may contain more useful information. Be clear about what you are prioritizing. Second, AI can introduce distortions. It may misinterpret tone, miss nuance, or overemphasize repeated or coordinated inputs, making some views appear more prevalent than they are. AI-generated outputs must be used with caution and should always be reviewed against the original input. Third, failing to account for practical constraints. If your evaluation criteria don't reflect what your organization can realistically implement, you risk elevating ideas that can't be acted on, raising expectations without delivering results. Evaluation requires both rigor and realism.
