# Video 8: Designing the Tasks: What You Ask Participants to Do

This video explains why task design determines whether participation produces usable results. It covers how to match tasks to goals, write clear and accessible instructions, and select platforms that support both participants and your team.

\[Visual description]: Title appears: Writing Clear Instructions.

Participants need to understand what is being asked of them and feel capable of doing it. Here are some tips. Prioritize clarity and accessibility. Avoid jargon. Instructions should match the literacy level and experience of your audience. Keep instructions short and easy to follow. Avoid long documents. Use brief text, simple visuals, or short videos, and ensure that your call to action is concise. Provide support or tutorials where needed. Define tasks precisely. Instead of broad questions, ask focused ones that prompt specific, actionable responses. The more specific the task, the more useful the output. Break complex tasks into simple steps. Clear, structured instructions make responses easier to compare and to use. Set expectations upfront. Explain why input is being collected, how it will be used, and when participants will hear back. Good instructions do more than just tell people what to do, they make people confident that they can do it well.

Once you've defined the task, the next step is to decide whether you need a digital engagement platform and, if so, which one. This decision has two sides to it. You need to design the experience for participants: how easy it is to take part, what they're asked to do, how they engage. But just as important is the experience for your team: how you receive, organize, and act on contributions. A single platform does not need to do everything. In many cases, input can be collected in one place and downloaded for analysis using other tools. What matters is having a clear plan for how you will use input.

\[Visual description]: Title appears: How You Can Use AI to Support Task Design.

AI can support task design in several practical ways. It can help refine your instructions. You can use AI to draft and test different versions of task prompts, simplify complex language, and adapt materials for different audiences. AI can expand how people contribute. Tools like speech-to-text, transcription, screen-reader support, all make it easier for people to participate regardless of literacy level, language, or ability. It can enable multilingual participation. Real-time translation allows people to engage in the language they are most comfortable using. AI can also help participants develop their ideas. By prompting with follow-up questions or suggesting ways to clarify input, AI can improve the quality of what people submit.

When used thoughtfully, AI makes tasks easier to complete and improves the quality of input without replacing human judgment.

\[Visual description]: Title appears: Risks in Task Design.

Getting task design wrong can undermine the entire engagement. Vague tasks produce unusable input. When participants do not know what's being asked of them, responses become broad or off-topic. Misaligned tasks waste effort. If the task does not match the goal, even thoughtful contributions will not support decision-making. AI can also distort the input you receive. If tools are too directive, you risk collecting AI-generated responses rather than people's own thinking. AI should help organize and summarize what people say, not substitute its own interpretation for theirs. Poorly designed tasks lead to lost time, frustrated participants, and eroded trust.
