# Video 4: Ensuring Ownership, Accountability and Action

This video examines who should be accountable for designing, running, and acting on engagement processes. It introduces three criteria for picking the right owner — trust, reach, and authority to act — and explains how you can use AI to expand who is able to lead engagement.

\[Visual description]: Title appears: Learning Goals.

In this video, you'll learn why ownership and accountability are essential. The importance of ownership in AI-enabled engagement. How to define roles so that community input leads to real decisions. Let's dive in.

Every project needs clear ownership, someone to design the process and ensure results lead to action. These may be the same or different entities. Without it, even well-intentioned efforts drift, stall, or fade. Ownership can sit inside or outside government or be shared among multiple partners. What matters is that everyone involved understands who is responsible for doing what.

\[Visual description]: Title appears: How to Pick The Right Owner.

When deciding who should own an engagement, consider three criteria. First, whom do people trust? Participation depends on credibility. If the community doesn't trust the institution running the process, they won't engage. Second, who has the ability to get the word out? Reaching people requires networks, relationships, and communications capacity. The owner needs to either have that reach or partner with organizations that do. Third, who can coordinate? Someone needs to manage relationships among partners, align responsibilities, and ensure accountability doesn't fall through the gaps. And finally, who can act on the input? This might be an institution with the power to implement. Or, it could be an organization that uses public input to advocate for change, putting pressure on decisionmakers to act. What matters is that someone is committed to doing something meaningful with the results.

\[Visual description]: Taiwan’s skyline is shown.

Taiwan's JOIN platform, launched in 2015 and still in use today, shows how this can work at scale.

\[Visual description]: The JOIN platform’s website is shown.

Run by the National Development Council, it enables citizens to propose ideas and shape policy. When a petition reaches 5,000 signatures, the responsible agency must respond, helping drive participation by millions. The initiative works because the owners are trusted, can reach participants, and can act on the results.

\[Visual description]: Title appears: Ownership in AI-Enabled Engagement.

When AI tools are part of the engagement, ownership takes on additional dimensions. When we can use AI to reduce the time and cost of engagement, it creates opportunities for otherwise under-resourced organizations to take ownership and run the engagement. At the same time, ownership includes responsibility for selecting the right platform and accountability for how AI-generated outputs will be reviewed.

\[Visual description]: Title appears: Risks To Watch For.

Questions of ownership raise two risks. First, diffuse ownership creates accountability gaps: when no one is responsible for turning input into decisions, engagement stalls between collection and action, rendering participation toothless for institutions and for the public. Second, misaligned ownership undermines impact: even with a designated owner, if they lack authority or a credible plan to use the results, participation becomes performative.
