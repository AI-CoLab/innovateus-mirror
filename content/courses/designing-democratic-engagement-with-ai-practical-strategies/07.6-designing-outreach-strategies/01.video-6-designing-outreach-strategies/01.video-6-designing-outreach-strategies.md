# Video 6: Designing Outreach Strategies

This video covers how to combine online and in-person outreach methods to reach the people your engagement is designed to involve. It explains how you can use AI tools to translate materials, generate tailored communications, and identify who is missing from the conversation.

\[Visual description]: Title appears: Outreach Strategies.

Some communities can be reached online through email, social media, websites or messaging platforms. Online outreach is often quicker, more cost-efficient and easier to scale, but it can also exclude those with limited internet access, lower digital literacy or lower levels of trust. For communities you can't reach online, you'll need to go where they are. In-person outreach through community events, public meetings, door-to-door canvassing. This reaches people who are often left out of digital processes. Think about how to tailor your methods to the tools and channels people already use to communicate.

\[Visual description]: Open plains in Malawi are shown.

In 2015, when floods displaced 200,000 people across Malawi, the World Bank used mobile phone surveys to collect information about the disaster's impact from thousands of households.

\[Visual description]: An animated cellphone tower sends a signal activating people’s phones.

By reaching people through a device they already had in their pockets, authorities could assess which communities were hardest hit and prioritize emergency response faster than traditional surveys would have allowed.

\[Visual description]: The EURHOPE initiative Final Report with its individual sections appears.

Reach people in their language. The EurHope initiative, which engaged 1.5 million people across the EU to create a youth-focused political agenda, made its entire platform including proposals, votes and results available in 22 languages.

Build outreach partnerships with organizations. Working through trusted institutions, community groups, libraries, faith organizations, schools, and local nonprofits. This can amplify your message, build trust in the process, and lower the barriers that keep people from participating.

Finally, remember that communication is a continuous process. Outreach doesn't end when participation begins. Following up to show how participant input was used is what transforms a one-time interaction into a culture of civic engagement. The principle is the same whether you're working at the national or the neighborhood level. Don't wait for people to come to you. Instead, think about how you can go to them.

\[Visual description]: Title appears: Using AI to Improve Outreach.

There are several ways AI can accelerate and expand outreach. You can use AI-powered chatbots to guide people through the process.

\[Visual description]: An Indonesian town appears.

In Indonesia, the PetaBencana platform uses a Telegram chatbot to make it easier to report flooding.

\[Visual description]: The GovLab project page for “PetaBencana (Risk Map)” appears.

\[Visual description]: A person is making a report with rescue personnel by taking a picture.

When someone mentions the word “banjir” or “flood” and tags the account, the chatbot prompts them to submit a report. You can also use AI to identify who is missing from the conversation. By analyzing social media activity, you can map which groups are most visible in public discourse and which are absent, helping you target outreach more deliberately. You can use AI to produce multiple versions of outreach materials tailored to different audiences, translate content into multiple languages and simplify jargon into plain language, making it easy to create a communications toolkit that partner organizations can share across their own networks.

\[Visual description]: A project webpage appears titled: How New Jersey Asked Workers about the Future of Work: The Importance of Partnership.

\[Visual description]: Title appears: Risks to Watch For.

Let's talk about a few risks that deserve attention as you design your outreach. First, style matters. Messaging that feels bureaucratic, impersonal, or culturally unfamiliar can dissuade people from engaging. Second, AI makes mistakes. Be sure to carefully check any text, data, or images created with AI for accuracy and thoroughly test any chatbots used. Finally, when using AI for translation, be aware of its limits. Outputs can lack cultural nuance and perform unevenly across languages, especially in languages with limited training data. Some governments are addressing this problem by building better language infrastructure.
