# Video 7: Designing Incentives for Participation

This video distinguishes between intrinsic motivators — such as civic purpose and visible impact — and extrinsic rewards, and explains why the strongest incentive is showing people how their input will be used. It also covers how to reduce barriers through practical support and how you can use AI to reinforce incentives by making contributions visible in real time

\[Visual description]: Title appears: Understanding Incentives.

Incentives can be extrinsic or intrinsic. Intrinsic motivators appeal to people's sense of purpose.

\[Visual description]: The Rio skyline is shown.

Brazil's Chamber of Deputies uses the e-Democracia platform to invite residents to debate and comment on draft legislation.

\[Visual description]: The e-Democracia platform website is shown.

Legislators publicly acknowledge when input shapes bills, reinforcing intrinsic motivations such as civic duty. Incentives can also be extrinsic. For example, the U.S. federal government's Challenge.gov rewards people with cash prizes.

\[Visual description]: The Challenge.gov website is shown.

While financial incentives may boost participation in the short term, intrinsic motivations are more effective for sustaining engagement. Effective designs often combine both, using external rewards to attract participants while building longer-term motivation through ownership and visible impact.

The strongest incentive, however, is showing people how their input will be used. College Station, Texas, is one of hundreds of cities where the SeeClickFix app lets residents report local issues and tracks their resolution, with nearly 20,000 fixes completed.

\[Visual description]: The College Station website is shown. It moves to the City of College Station Blog article titled: The free and easy SeeClickFix app has resolved nearly 20,000 citizen-reported issues.

That visible connection between input and action encourages people to participate again and to encourage others to do the same. The most powerful motivator is the experience of having made a real difference.

\[Visual description]: Title appears: Reducing Barriers and Enabling Change.

Designing incentives is also about removing barriers. Practical support like childcare, transportation, or compensation can make participation possible, especially for those who cannot afford to volunteer their time. Reducing barriers also means helping people contribute. Residents may have strong ideas but lack experience presenting them. Providing training, coaching, and feedback ensures the quality of input reflects the strength of those ideas.

\[Visual description]: The City of Lakewood, Colorado is shown.

In Lakewood, the city paired incentives with technical advice, and help securing funding so residents could turn ideas into action.

\[Visual description]: Title appears: Risks to Watch For.

Two risks deserve attention. First, incentives can attract bad actors. When money or influence is at stake, coordinated campaigns may submit duplicate or fabricated input, often called astroturfing. Clear rules, deduplication, and human review can help mitigate this risk. Second, and most important, is follow-through. If you offer compensation, provide it. If you promise recognition, make it meaningful. And above all, use the input you collect. When participants feel their time was not valued, they do not return, and trust erodes quickly.

\[Visual description]: Title appears: How AI Can Be Used to Help.

AI tools can reinforce incentives in several ways. You can use AI to make contributions visible in real time.

\[Visual description]: A shopping street and a residential street in Derry/Londonderry, Northern Ireland are shown.

In Northern Ireland, the Voice Matters initiative used AI to transcribe face-to-face deliberations and generate real-time visualizations of participants' ideas, giving people immediate, visible evidence that their contributions were shaping the discussion. When using AI to support incentives, the goal is to amplify participation, not replace it. AI should make human contributions more visible and meaningful, enhancing legitimacy and building trust.
