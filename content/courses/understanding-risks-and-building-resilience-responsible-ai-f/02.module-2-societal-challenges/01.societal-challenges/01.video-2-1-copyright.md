# Video 2.1 | Copyright

Broader societal risks and uncertainties surrounding GenAI are introduced. The video focuses on copyright issues, discussing the lack of protection for AI-generated works and the unsettled question of using copyrighted material for training GenAI models.

Deepfakes

Private ownership

*\[Visual description]:* Title card: Copyright.

What remains especially contested is whether GenAI companies have the right to use other people’s content to train their models in the first place.

*\[Visual description]:* Image appears: a screenshot of The New York Times article titled: The Times Sues OpenAI and Microsoft Over A.I. Use of Copyrighted Work.

 As of this recording, dozens of copyright lawsuits have been filed against AI companies in the United States—and that number continues to grow.

*\[Visual description]:* Image appears: a screenshot of the Reuters article titled: Disney, Universal sue image creator Midjourney for copyright infringement.

A large portion of training data comes from public domain or openly licensed sources, such as government publications and Wikipedia.

*\[Visual description]:* The logos of the National Archives, the DPLA, and Wikipedia are shown.

But concerns arise with privately owned content. Some platforms have entered licensing agreements to provide data for AI training. Others have taken legal action—for example, the New York Times has sued Microsoft and OpenAI over the use of its articles, and courts have allowed key parts of that case to move forward.

*\[Visual description]:* Image appears: a screenshot of the NPR article titled: Judge allows ‘New York Times’ copyright case against OpenAI to go forward.

Other disputes have resulted in major settlements. The legal landscape is evolving quickly.

In 2025, the U.S. Copyright Office issued guidance indicating that some uses of copyrighted material for AI training may qualify as fair use, while others may not.

*\[Visual description]:* Image appears: a screenshot of an article titled: United States Copyright Office weighs in on fair use defense for Generative AI Training.

These questions are likely to be resolved through ongoing litigation on a case-by-case basis.

In Europe, the 2024 AI Act requires providers of general-purpose AI systems to publish summaries of their training data, so that copyright holders can better understand and enforce their rights.

*\[Visual description]:* Image appears: a screenshot of the EU AI Act. A specific section of the Transparency requirements is highlighted: “Publishing summaries of copyrighted data used for training.”

*\[Visual description]:* Title card: AI governance shapes.

How this is resolved will shape what data AI systems can be trained on—and, in turn, the quality and capabilities of these tools.

Next, we'll look at deepfakes, astroturfing, and the perils of private ownership.
