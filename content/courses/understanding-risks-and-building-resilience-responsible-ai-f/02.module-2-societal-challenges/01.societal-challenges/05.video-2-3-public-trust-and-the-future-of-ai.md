### Audio description transcript - Public Trust and the Future of AI

*\[Visual description]:* Text appears: The InnovateUS Logo.

*\[Visual description]:* Title card: Public Trust and the Future of AI.

*\[Visual description]:* Text appears: Ariana Escanlante, presenter, InnovateUS.

As public servants, our core mission is to act in the best interests of the people we serve.

Governments worldwide have embedded this mission into their AI frameworks. Among Ohio's five principles for responsible AI: any use must be "Human-Centric and Socially Beneficial."

*\[Visual description]:* Ohio.govs’ logo is visible next to the 5 principles: Fair, Accountable, Secure and safe, Explainable and Transparent, and Human-centric and socially beneficial.

The City of Boston calls for centering "Public Purpose" — ensuring service to the public stays at the center of every decision.

*\[Visual description]:* The City of Boston logo is visible next to its AI principles: Empowerment, Inclusion and respect, Transparency and accountability, Innovation and risk management, Privacy and security, and Public purpose.

 The EU calls for ensuring AI use provides for "societal and environmental well-being."

*\[Visual description]:* The EU Flag is visible next to its 7 AI requirements: Human agency and oversight, Technical robustness and safety, Privacy and data governance, Transparency, Diversity, Non-discrimination and fairness, Societal and environmental well-being, and Accountability.

These principles must translate into tangible actions: identifying and addressing challenges facing our communities, actively seeking public and worker input throughout the development and deployment of AI solutions, and establishing robust feedback and accountability systems that allow residents to evaluate their effectiveness.

Just because we can do something with technology does not mean we should do it.

*\[Visual description]:* Image appears: a signpost with a trail map standing at a split on a trail, showing potential paths to take. Generated by ChatGPT.

As public professionals, you occupy a unique position in the AI landscape. You are not just potential users of these tools — you are also the stewards of public trust. The decisions your agencies make about how and when to deploy AI will shape whether residents experience these technologies as empowering or alienating. That's a responsibility worth taking seriously.

This means pushing back when AI is proposed for uses where it isn't ready — or where the stakes of an error are too high.

*\[Visual description]:* Image appears: a city being overlayed by a neon computer grid labeled CITY OS 4.7. Generated by ChatGPT.

 It means asking hard questions about accountability: if a GenAI system helps make a decision that harms a resident, who is responsible? It means advocating within your organization for clear guidelines, training, and oversight mechanisms before AI tools are deployed at scale.

It also means being curious and engaged. The professionals who shape how AI is used in government will largely be people who know both the work and the tools — people who can spot an opportunity, and also spot a risk. That's the knowledge this course has been building toward.

The pace of change means the tools you use a year from now may look quite different from what we've covered here.

Two developments on the horizon are worth learning more about.

The first is agentic AI. Generative AI and agentic AI differ in how work gets done.

*\[Visual description]:* The logos of Gumloop, Claude, N8n, [Relay.app](http://Relay.app), Cursor, Zapier, Crewai, and StackAI are displayed.

*\[Visual description]:* Text appears: Generative AI.

With Generative AI, you direct each step: you ask a question, review the response, and decide what to do next. The AI generates content, but you remain in control of each step of the work.

*\[Visual description]:* Text appears: Agentic AI.

With agentic AI, you define the goal and the AI carries out a sequence of actions to achieve it. For example, if asking an agent to help you with a policy memo, it might research a topic, pull relevant documents, draft the text, and flag questions for your review. Agents raise new questions about accountability and oversight.

The second is predictive AI.

*\[Visual description]:* The logos of DOMO, Azure, SAS Viya, and [H20.ai](http://H20.ai) are displayed.

Systems that analyze patterns in historical data to forecast outcomes. Governments are already exploring this for infrastructure maintenance, budget forecasting, and identifying residents at risk of losing critical services. Used well, predictive AI helps agencies get ahead of problems rather than just respond to them. Used carelessly, it can automate bias at scale.

The fundamentals this course covers — evaluating outputs, spotting hallucinations, thinking through bias, maintaining human oversight — transfer directly to these more advanced tools. Understanding how a language model works makes it much easier to audit an agent's actions or interrogate a predictive model's assumptions.

Knowing the fundamentals you learned here will make you better able to know how to test how different tools perform. Benchmarking which tool is most accurate for your policy domain? Which hallucinates least on questions your constituents actually ask? These are questions your organization will need to answer.

*\[Visual description]:* Text appears: What you’ll need (whatever comes next).

Both agents and predictive AI represent real opportunities — and real responsibilities. The habits of critical thinking, human oversight, and ethical judgment this course has been building are exactly what you'll need for whatever comes next.
